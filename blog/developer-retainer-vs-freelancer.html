Do You Need a Developer on Retainer, a Freelancer, or an Agency?

For many small and mid-sized businesses, the hard part is not knowing whether software would help. The hard part is figuring out who should build it, how much help you actually need, and how to avoid paying for a giant project that stalls halfway through.

Nerd On Retainer · 7 min read

If your business needs a simple one-time task, a freelancer can be perfect. If you need a large fixed-scope build with a full team, an agency can make sense. But if your company keeps needing websites, dashboards, payment integrations, automations, portals, and ongoing improvements, a developer on retainer can be the cleaner middle path.

The freelancer problem

Freelancers are great when the job is clearly defined: build this landing page, fix this checkout bug, connect this form, update this plugin. The challenge shows up when your business needs steady product thinking and ongoing support. A freelancer may be busy, unavailable, unfamiliar with your stack, or gone by the time the next urgent problem shows up.

That does not make freelancing bad. It just means the model works best for contained work, not for becoming your long-term software department.

The agency problem

Agencies can bring design, strategy, development, project management, and QA under one roof. That is valuable, but it can also be expensive and slow for everyday business software needs. Many businesses do not need a giant discovery phase every time they want to improve a customer portal or automate a manual process.

If you need a six-figure custom platform with multiple specialists, an agency may be right. If you need consistent execution month after month, a retainer may be more practical.

The full-time hire problem

A full-time developer sounds attractive until you add salary, taxes, benefits, recruiting time, management, equipment, and the risk of hiring the wrong person. A capable developer can easily cost far more than the salary number on paper, and many small businesses do not have enough focused development work to keep that person fully utilized all year.

Where a development retainer fits

A monthly development retainer works best when your business has an ongoing queue of improvements. Maybe you need a better website this month, Stripe subscriptions next month, a staff dashboard after that, then an automation to remove a spreadsheet your team hates. The work keeps coming, but not always in a neat one-time project shape.

Good retainer work includes

  • Custom websites and landing pages
  • Customer portals and employee dashboards
  • Payment systems and billing integrations
  • AI automation and business process automation
  • API integrations between tools you already use
  • Mobile app planning and phased builds
  • Maintenance, fixes, and ongoing improvements

The rule that keeps it fair

Submit as many requests as you want. We work through them one at a time. That gives the business freedom to keep adding ideas without turning the retainer into chaos. It also protects quality because the developer is not pretending to build five things at once.

When a retainer is not right

A retainer is probably not the right fit if you only need one tiny update per year, if you already have a strong internal software team, or if every project needs a large specialized team working in parallel. It is also not a magic button. Bigger builds still need sequencing, priorities, testing, and clear feedback.

The practical answer

If your business has ongoing software needs but does not want to hire a full-time developer, a retainer can be a very practical middle ground. You get continuity, speed, and someone who learns how your business actually works.
